01

Background

Context

Room Selection on apps had accumulated years of complexity. Each room–rate combination was rendered as a separate card, creating visual repetition and making it hard for users to compare options. By 2024, the Room List hosted components from multiple teams — a system that had grown without a clear information architecture to govern it.

During H1, the track ran a design sprint to explore alternative Room Selection approaches. I worked on two concepts (Filters and 2-Step Rate Selection) before the track converged on Horizontal Comparison as the strategic direction for H2.

I took over full ownership of Horizontal Comparison for Apps in H2. The mobile web version, which originated with another designer on the team, was still in progress at that point and had not shipped.

[Image: HC interaction overview — horizontal scroll to compare rates within a room, vertical scroll to browse rooms. Multiple rate selection of the same type was excluded from the first iteration to keep the interaction focused.]

Problem

The existing Room List presented every room and rate as an individual card. For properties with multiple rate options per room, this created:

  • Repeated information across cards (room photos, amenities, descriptions)
  • No clear hierarchy between room-level and rate-level content
  • Poor comparability: users couldn't easily evaluate rates within the same room

Research across the five H1 concepts found no major pain points with Horizontal Comparison specifically, though some users struggled to discover that the rate carousel was horizontally scrollable. We added a tracking metric to the A/B experiment to monitor scrollability adoption.

The secondary challenge was scale. Any structural change to the Room List touched ~100 components. A single large experiment would make it nearly impossible to isolate what drove results, or what caused harm.

Laying the Groundwork

Component Audit

I conducted a comprehensive audit of ~100 components across Room List and Room Details. The key question was whether each component was room-level (e.g. "Free cot available") or rate-level (e.g. "Free cancellation") — a distinction that mattered because Horizontal Comparison groups multiple rates under a single room card.

Mapping ownership early allowed me to communicate upcoming changes before decisions were locked, discuss solutions to potential blockers, and work with teams to adapt their in-progress designs to the new flow.

[Image: Component audit screenshot]

Block-out Experiments

Block-out experiments test the removal of an existing feature rather than the introduction of something new. They reveal the real value a component is driving — often invisible until you take it away — and are critical for informing both design and business strategy.

Before redesigning every component, I ran blockouts across the Room List to assess which elements were worth keeping. This resulted in removing 12+ components with no meaningful impact on conversion or user behaviour, including the Secret deal banner, "Tip: this room is bigger than most", Lowest price badge, Just booked, Booked before, and Thai pass, among others.

One component that could not be removed was Jackpot. Data showed removing it would cost 508 ±219 PNC. I redesigned it as a compact list item, preserving conversion without occupying disproportionate space in the new Rate Card.

IA Experiments

I identified a long-standing IA debt: room and rate information lacked a clear order across both platforms, and Android and iOS had diverged over time. I defined experiments to establish a consistent hierarchy and align the two platforms before introducing Horizontal Comparison.

03

Execution

Cross-Team Alignment

I organized an initial presentation to brief Families, Pricing, Genius, Genius VIP, and Booking Policies on the upcoming changes, then followed up directly with individual designers and PMs. Two alignment cases were particularly consequential:

Genius Free Room Upgrade

Adapting this component increased user confusion, developers flagged high complexity, and conversion data showed negative impact. With those three signals aligned, we made the call to exclude it from the first iteration and coordinate partner communication before sunsetting it.

Room Details restructuring

Horizontal Comparison introduced a single Room Details screen per room rather than per room–rate combination. Genius agreed to remove their banner, as the information remained accessible in the Rate Carousel. Booking Policies merged their content block into the Rate Carousel.

Horizontal Comparison: MVP Experiment

With the foundation in place, the core Horizontal Comparison experiment launched with a clean starting point.

The primary metric was booked non-cheapest rate. Our hypothesis was that users were defaulting to the cheapest option not out of preference, but because the existing design made it hard to understand the value of more expensive alternatives. A clearer, side-by-side rate view would lead to more informed selections.

The experiment full-oned on both Android and iOS. (Primary metric result to be added.)

Tracking showed over 75% of Android users scrolled horizontally, addressing the discoverability concern surfaced in research.

Parallel Strategies: How Two Teams Approached the Same Problem

The phased roadmap turned out to be one of the most important structural decisions of the project — not because it made individual experiments more likely to succeed, but because it made failure easier to learn from. The roadmap wasn't a guarantee. It was a way of staying in control of a project that had every reason to spiral.

Having two teams working on the same hypothesis across different platforms allowed for a natural strategic split: Mobile Web could move fast and test HC as a full experience, while Apps could take a more measured approach and collect more isolated learnings. Both approaches have value, as speed of validation and clarity of signal serve different purposes. Throughout the process, both teams stayed in close collaboration, sharing findings and working toward the same goal: helping users find and select the room that best fits their needs.

05

Reflection

What I'd Tackle Next

Horizontal Comparison changed the relationship between Room Details and rate information. With HC, Room Details became rate-agnostic, so rate-specific content that previously lived there now needs a clear home. The next logical step is a cross-platform experiment consolidating rate information into a single, structured location.

A parallel priority would be resolving UI inconsistencies across the Room List. Several teams (Genius, B.Home, T. Policies, SEGA) had implemented their own components with differing icon sizes and spacing. Aligning these before the next major iteration would reduce visual noise and improve overall coherence.

Reflection

This project was the most complex stakeholder environment I'd worked in at Booking.com: six teams, two platforms, ~100 components, and a hypothesis that had already struggled to ship elsewhere.

The decision to audit and de-risk before experimenting came from recognizing that a failed experiment on a cluttered baseline would tell us almost nothing. The sequencing mattered as much as the design.

If I were doing it again, I'd push earlier for a clearer answer on the Families use case. Excluding traffic solves a short-term problem but creates a gap that compounds over iterations.

It's easy to underestimate foundational work. IA experiments were initially seen as debt cleanup, and the apps adaptation was regularly referred to in meetings as a port. I was asked more than once whether I was simply copying the web design as closely as possible. Both ended up producing the most concrete results. This project reinforced my belief that foundational decisions, even unglamorous ones, are often where the real impact lives.
