Room Selection Apps · 2024

Horizontal Comparison

Redesigning Room Selection to reduce choice overload and improve comparability

Role Senior Product Designer
Platforms Android · iOS
Scope Strategy · Design · Experimentation
Timeframe 2024
Problem

Room Selection on apps presented every room–rate combination as a separate card, creating visual repetition, poor comparability, and choice overload. The Room List had grown to ~100 components across 6+ teams with no clear information architecture governing it.

Approach

After a track-wide H1 sprint exploring five Room Selection concepts (including a Horizontal Comparison design created for mobile web by another designer) we converged on HC as the strategic direction. I took ownership of adapting and shipping it on Android and iOS across three phases: Foundation work, MVP experiment, and future iterations.

01

Background

Context

During H1, the track ran a design sprint to explore alternative Room Selection approaches. I worked on two concepts (Filters and 2-Step Rate Selection) before the track converged on Horizontal Comparison as the strategic direction for H2.

I took over full ownership of Horizontal Comparison for Apps in H2. The mobile web version, which originated with another designer on the team, was still in progress at that point and had not shipped.

Problem

Room Selection suffered from two distinct problems.

The findability problem: properties offered too many room-rate combinations, each presented as a separate vertical card. With some properties offering over 16 options, most users never explored beyond the first few.

The comparability problem: many rooms shared the same amenities and size, giving users no reliable way to understand what differentiated one rate from another. When users could not find meaningful differences between the cheapest option and higher-value rooms, they settled for the first one they saw. Roughly 65% of users defaulted to booking the cheapest option. In post-checkout research, around 30% of users said they believed they had booked a free cancellation rate when they had not.

During H1 we experimented with 5 design solutions. Horizontal Rate Scrolling was the most successful.

This structure reduced risk by avoiding simultaneous variable changes and gave the team a framework for deferring complexity rather than letting it block progress. It also meant that even if the core HC hypothesis hadn't worked, the foundational experiments would have still delivered measurable improvements to Base.

Laying the Groundwork

Component Audit

I conducted a comprehensive audit of ~100 components across Room List and Room Details. The key question was whether each component was room-level (e.g. "Free cot available") or rate-level (e.g. "Free cancellation") — a distinction that mattered because Horizontal Comparison groups multiple rates under a single room card.

Mapping ownership early allowed me to communicate upcoming changes before decisions were locked, discuss solutions to potential blockers, and work with teams to adapt their in-progress designs to the new flow.

Block-out Experiments

HC was an opportunity to clean up Room Selection from components added years earlier. As owner of the component database, I identified 12 candidates for removal — anything we didn't have to redesign or migrate to the new tech stack was a win before the main experiment even ran. This resulted in removing 12+ components with no meaningful impact on conversion, including the Secret deal banner, Lowest price badge, Just booked, and Booked before, among others.

One component that could not be removed was Jackpot. Data showed removing it would cost 508±219 PNC. I redesigned it as a compact list item, preserving conversion without occupying disproportionate space in the new Rate Card.

Color Alignment

Breakfast "included" and "available" used different color logic across platforms — green on one, black on the other. Before testing HC, I proposed aligning them for two reasons: avoid confounded results between platforms, and reduce unnecessary green usage. Teams had been running multivariate tests where green almost always won — so everything became green. If everything signals value, nothing does. I aligned the colors before HC shipped, confirmed no revenue impact, and established a reusable principle for what green should mean in Room Selection.

IA Experiments

I identified a long-standing IA debt: room and rate information lacked a clear order across both platforms, and Android and iOS had diverged over time. I defined experiments to establish a consistent hierarchy and align the two platforms before introducing Horizontal Comparison.

The structural change operated on two levels. At the macro level, the room list was reorganised so each room card contained its rates horizontally, reducing the total number of vertical cards. The room details screen was restructured accordingly, with rate information consolidated inside the carousel and room information moved below.

At the micro level, the order of elements within each room card was aligned with experiments run on both platforms. A clearer information hierarchy proved its value: the Android experiment returned +629±763 PNC and iOS +375±244 PNC, confirming that IA improvements alone, without visual redesign, could move conversion. The rate card was a different story. The gap between web and apps was too large to address in one experiment, and ownership was fragmented — each element belonged to a different team, with no agreed logic for prioritisation. We scoped it out deliberately and flagged it as a future iteration post-fullon.

03

Execution

Cross-Team Alignment

Six teams were affected by the redesign. Two alignment cases were particularly consequential:

Families and Policies

Family traffic was excluded from the first iteration after I couldn't reach alignment with Families and Policies teams on where to surface family rate details in the new IA. I worked on two tracks in parallel: a short-term solution to unblock iteration 2, and ongoing discussions toward a longer-term vision of consolidating all rate details into the Booking Conditions screen. The short-term solution landed for iteration 2. The longer-term vision had gained support from both teams and from Web by the time I left.

Jackpot

Jackpot is a component that appears after a user has selected a room, before proceeding to checkout. The component audit identified it as rate-level, meaning it needed a place in the new Rate Card. Before redesigning it, I ran a blockout experiment to confirm it was actually driving value. Removing it cost -508±219 PNC, significant enough to keep. I redesigned it as a compact list item that preserved conversion without occupying disproportionate space in the new Rate Card.

Room Details restructuring

Horizontal Comparison introduced a single Room Details screen per room rather than per room–rate combination. Genius agreed to remove their banner, as the information remained accessible in the Rate Carousel. Booking Policies merged their content block into the Rate Carousel.

Horizontal Comparison: MVP Experiment

Horizontal Comparison restructured the room selection experience around a single principle: split the room from the rate. Instead of presenting every room-rate combination as a separate vertical card, each room card now contained its rates in a horizontally scrollable carousel. Users could first find and compare rooms by scrolling vertically, then compare rates within a room by scrolling horizontally. A dedicated selection details screen consolidated customisation choices — meals, cancellation policies, beds — and future value-add options like spa or tours.

iOS followed the web interaction model: the entire rate card was tappable, with the exception of some info elements and minimal badges. On Android, I took the opportunity to also test a more conservative variant where only the CTA and radio button were tappable, closer to how the previous room card had behaved. Testing both was deliberate: the fully tappable card had already proven itself on web, but since it was a departure from the existing app interaction, shipping it without testing would have meant introducing two changes at once. The fully tappable card won on both Android and iOS, confirming the web behaviour translated to apps.

Getting to Fullon: Four iOS Iterations

iOS required four iterations before fullon. Family traffic was identified as a risk early. During the component audit and prework phase, I drafted multiple ways we could incorporate Families content into the new Room details IA.

I presented the alternatives to Families and Policies teams, but couldn't reach alignment. I had proposed consolidating all rate details into the Booking Conditions screen, which Families team supported. Policies pushed back. They owned the screen, had run their own experiments optimizing it, and adding family content risked competing for attention with theirs. Without agreement, we excluded family traffic from the first iteration, our MVP, to avoid delaying the core hypothesis test.

In parallel, I worked on two fronts: a short-term solution to unblock iteration 2, and ongoing discussions to align on a longer-term IA vision. The short-term solution, a one-line summary with an info icon, landed for iteration 2. The longer-term vision had gained support from Policies, Families, and Web by the time I left. Subsequent iterations refined tap targets, thumbnail size, and performance before meeting fullon criteria on iteration 4.

Results: Android

The Android experiment full-oned after 9 days. The primary metric, booked non-cheapest rate, increased by +4.54% (V1) and +4.82% (V2), representing +3,118±415 additional bookings per day. PNC was non-inferior.

Over 86% of Android users scrolled horizontally, validating the core discoverability hypothesis that had been flagged as a risk in research. Traffic to the room details screen increased by over 20%, suggesting users were exploring more before booking rather than defaulting to the first option they saw.

One known trade-off: as we deprioritised the use case where customers can book multiple rooms with different rates, bookings for that use case dropped 8.29%. This was acknowledged and flagged as a future iteration.

Business metrics improved on multiple levels, showing this experiment was not only an improvement for customers, but also for Booking.com and its partners.

Results: iOS

The iOS experiment required five weeks of runtime plus a two-week measurement period before going fullon. The primary metric, booked non-cheapest rate, increased by +2.91%±0.22%, representing +5,614±415 additional bookings per day. PNC was non-inferior, with a small overrun on the accepted cost threshold reviewed and approved by leadership given the opportunity cost of further delay.

Commission impact took longer to materialise than on Android, becoming conclusive at six weeks of data, consistent with the pattern seen across both platforms.

As with Android, business metrics improved across multiple levels — a win for customers, Booking.com, and partners.

Combined Impact

The three phases of work built on each other deliberately. Foundational work, including the component audit, blockout experiments, and color alignment, created a clean baseline. Without it, any experiment result would have been harder to interpret and any negative signal harder to isolate.

The IA experiments came next, establishing a consistent information hierarchy across both platforms before the main hypothesis was tested. They delivered measurable value independently: +629±763 PNC on Android and +375±244 PNC on iOS, confirming that IA improvements alone, without visual redesign, could move conversion.

With the foundation in place and the IA validated, the core Horizontal Comparison experiment launched with the clearest possible signal. It full-oned on both platforms, increasing the rate of users booking non-cheapest options by +4.54% on Android and +2.91% on iOS. Users were no longer defaulting to the first and cheapest option, but finding and selecting the room that actually fit their needs.

05

Reflection

What I'd Tackle Next

Horizontal Comparison changed the relationship between Room Details and rate information. With HC, Room Details became rate-agnostic, so rate-specific content that previously lived there now needs a clear home. The next logical step is a cross-platform experiment consolidating rate information into a single, structured location.

A parallel priority would be resolving UI inconsistencies across the Room List. Several teams (Genius, B.Home, T. Policies, SEGA) had implemented their own components with differing icon sizes and spacing. Aligning these before the next major iteration would reduce visual noise and improve overall coherence.

Reflection

This project was the most complex stakeholder environment I'd worked in at Booking.com: six teams, two platforms, ~100 components, and a hypothesis that had already struggled to ship elsewhere.

The decision to audit and de-risk before experimenting came from recognizing that a failed experiment on a cluttered baseline would tell us almost nothing. The sequencing mattered as much as the design.

If I were doing it again, I'd push earlier for a clearer answer on the Families use case. Excluding traffic solves a short-term problem but creates a gap that compounds over iterations.

It's easy to underestimate foundational work. IA experiments were initially seen as debt cleanup, and the apps adaptation was regularly referred to in meetings as a port. I was asked more than once whether I was simply copying the web design as closely as possible. Both ended up producing the most concrete results. This project reinforced my belief that foundational decisions, even unglamorous ones, are often where the real impact lives.
